How Much Margin Do You Need for 1 Lot of Gold?

How much margin for 1 lot of gold? XAUUSD margin = lots × 100 oz × price ÷ leverage. Tables for 1:20 to 1:1000, margin level and stop out.

How much margin for 1 lot of gold do you need? The answer depends on three things: the contract size, the current gold price and the leverage your broker gives you on metals. For a standard XAUUSD contract of 100 ounces at a price of 4,200, the margin for 1 lot ranges from a few hundred dollars at very high leverage to more than twenty thousand dollars at 1:20. This guide shows the formula, a full table of XAUUSD margin for 1 lot, 0.10 lot and 0.01 lot, and explains why margin is not the same thing as risk.

The margin formula

Margin is the deposit your broker sets aside while a position is open. It is not a fee and it is not spent; it is released when you close the trade. For gold, the calculation is:

Margin = lots × contract size × price ÷ leverage

Most brokers use a contract size of 100 troy ounces per 1.00 lot on XAUUSD. Some use different sizes, and some offer mini or cent contracts, so check the symbol specification in MT5 (right-click the symbol, then Specification) before relying on any number here.

Notional value comes first

Before leverage, work out the notional value: the full market value of the gold you control. At an example price of 4,200, one lot is 100 × 4,200 = 420,000 USD of gold. A 0.10 lot controls 42,000 USD and a 0.01 lot controls 4,200 USD. Leverage only decides what fraction of that notional the broker asks you to post as margin. At 1:100 you post one hundredth, so 420,000 ÷ 100 = 4,200 USD for one lot.

Your account leverage may not apply to gold

A common surprise is opening an account at 1:500 and finding that a gold trade uses more margin than expected. Many brokers set a separate, often lower, maximum leverage for metals than for major currency pairs. Some also lower leverage automatically as your position size or account equity grows, or around weekends and major news. The leverage shown on your account is a ceiling, not a promise for every symbol.

Regulation matters too. Under the EU retail rules, for example, gold is capped at 1:20 for retail clients, which means 21,000 USD of margin for one lot at 4,200. Other regulators have their own limits. Professional or offshore accounts may allow more, but higher leverage does not make a trade safer; it only reduces the deposit.

Margin level, margin call and stop out

MT5 shows a few figures in the Trade tab that are worth understanding:

  • Equity = balance plus or minus open profit and loss.
  • Used margin = the total margin held for open positions.
  • Free margin = equity minus used margin. This is what is available to open new trades or absorb losses.
  • Margin level = equity ÷ used margin × 100%.
Margin level % = equity ÷ used margin × 100

Brokers set a margin call level, where you get a warning, and a stop out level, where the platform starts closing positions automatically, usually the one with the largest loss first. Common values are a margin call at 100% and a stop out at 50% or lower, but these vary widely between brokers and account types, so read your broker's terms.

Example. You have 10,000 USD equity and open 1.00 lot of XAUUSD at 4,200 with 1:100 leverage. Used margin is 4,200, free margin is 5,800 and margin level is 10,000 ÷ 4,200 × 100 = about 238%. If the broker's margin call is at 100%, it is reached when equity falls to 4,200, which is a loss of 5,800, or a move of 58 dollars per ounce against you (5,800 ÷ 100). With a stop out at 50%, positions start closing when equity reaches 2,100, a loss of 7,900, or a 79-dollar move. These figures ignore spread, swaps and the small change in margin as price moves.

Margin is not risk

This is the most important point in the article. Margin tells you how big a position the broker lets you open. It says nothing about how much you can lose. Your risk on a trade comes from two numbers only: the distance to your stop loss and your lot size.

Risk (USD) = stop distance ($ per oz) × 100 × lots

A 0.10 lot trade with a 15-dollar stop risks 15 × 100 × 0.10 = 150 USD. That is true at 1:20 and at 1:1000. Higher leverage only frees up margin, which can tempt traders into lot sizes far larger than their stop justifies. A trader with 1,000 USD at 1:1000 could open 2.00 lots on 840 USD of margin, and a mere 1-dollar move against that position would lose 200 USD, or 20% of the account. To keep risk tied to the stop rather than the leverage, size positions with the gold lot size calculator and read the 1% rule with gold examples. For how each dollar of movement translates into money, see XAUUSD pip value explained.

Hedged positions. If you hold a buy and a sell on gold at the same time, brokers handle margin differently. Some charge margin only on the larger side, some charge a reduced hedged margin, and some charge both sides in full. Hedging also does not remove spread and swap costs. Check your broker's hedged margin setting before relying on it.

Practical checks before you trade

Look up the contract size and margin percentage in the symbol specification, confirm the metals leverage on your account, note the margin call and stop out levels, and make sure your free margin can comfortably cover normal price swings plus your stop. Key takeaways

  • Margin = lots × contract size × price ÷ leverage; for 1 lot at 4,200 and 1:100 that is 4,200 USD.
  • One lot of XAUUSD controls about 420,000 USD of gold at a 4,200 price; leverage only changes the deposit.
  • Metals leverage is often lower than FX leverage, and regulators such as the EU cap retail gold at 1:20.
  • Watch margin level, free margin and your broker's margin call and stop out levels.
  • Risk comes from stop distance and lot size, not from margin or leverage.

FAQ

How much margin do I need for 1 lot of gold at 1:100?

At an example price of 4,200 with a 100-ounce contract, the margin is 100 × 4,200 ÷ 100 = 4,200 USD. The figure moves with the gold price and with your broker's actual metals leverage.

How much margin is 0.01 lot of XAUUSD?

At 4,200 and 1:100 leverage, 0.01 lot needs about 42 USD of margin. At 1:500 it would be about 8.40 USD, and at 1:20 about 210 USD.

Why is my gold margin higher than my account leverage suggests?

Many brokers apply a separate, lower leverage to metals, or reduce leverage for larger positions or around news and weekends. Check the symbol specification and your broker's leverage table.

Does higher leverage mean higher risk?

Not directly, since the loss on a trade depends on lot size and stop distance. The danger is that high leverage lets you open lots that are too large for your account, which is where the real risk comes from.
